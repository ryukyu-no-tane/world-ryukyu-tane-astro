On July 8, 2026, Ryukyu no Tane representative Sadoyama joined a panel at the FY2026 General Meeting and Training Workshop of the Chubu District School Library Association, held at the Yomitan Village Cultural Center.

The theme was “Connecting Hearts and Expanding Learning Through School Libraries.” Around 200 teacher-librarians and school librarians from elementary and junior high schools across central Okinawa attended. The event was also featured in the July 8, 2026 edition of the Okinawa Times.

![Panel discussion at the school library workshop](https://news.ryukyu-tane.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/IMG_9163-1024x768.jpeg)

## Three Panelists Share Their Work

The panel brought together Yosuke Moriyama, president of the Onna Elementary School PTA and an active read-aloud volunteer; childcare professional and writer Naoko Henza; and a representative of Ryukyu no Tane.

Moriyama described ways of building mysteries into read-aloud activities so that children discover that “the library holds the answer.” His examples included changes over time in the pronunciation of Okinawan place names and surnames, as well as games involving words played backward. Each activity helps children experience how books can answer the everyday questions that spark their curiosity.

Henza shared a case that showed how a library can become a meaningful space for a child. A student who found interpersonal relationships difficult used the calm of the library as a refuge, completed a novel, and eventually exhibited it at a literary flea market. Reading can support a child’s sense of competence when it extends beyond consuming stories to writing and presenting work of their own.

## Connecting AI and Libraries in New Ways

Speaking as a learning-environment planner, Sadoyama proposed two practical ways to combine digital tools with libraries.

The first was **selecting books through the lens of multiple intelligences theory**. Instead of forcing a child to read, adults can first identify the forms of intelligence in which that child is strongest—such as spatial, bodily-kinesthetic, or musical intelligence—and then recommend books that connect with those interests.

The second was **using AI and the Nippon Decimal Classification (NDC) to find books**. A child might begin by asking an AI service such as ChatGPT to suggest books suited to them. The child can then ask the AI to review their conversation and identify the NDC number most likely to contain books related to their interests. The final and most important step is physical: going to the library and standing in front of that shelf.

This path links online reflection with offline discovery. Participants responded that they wanted to try the approach at their schools.

## Another Place of Belonging, Alongside the School Nurse’s Office

The discussion also explored the essential role of school libraries. Sadoyama and Henza suggested that, like the school nurse’s office, the library can be a place where children speak honestly without worrying about evaluation. For students who are struggling to attend school, a library is more than a place that stores books; it can be a safe place to spend time.

Other examples included how Japanese often omits the grammatical subject, which can create a barrier to reading for children from other countries, and a family custom called “otoshibon,” in which a child receives one book of their choice at New Year without having to worry about the price. These stories led to a discussion of reading initiatives that make thoughtful use of both digital and analog tools.

## Reflections from Participants

Satsuki Arakaki, a librarian at Kitanakagusuku Elementary School, spoke about what she had learned and her hope that libraries will connect children, teachers, and the wider community, as reported by the Okinawa Times. The moderator concluded that libraries are important places of connection not only for children, but also for adults and communities. The session closed with warm applause.
